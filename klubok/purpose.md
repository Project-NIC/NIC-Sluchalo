# Klubok — the purpose

*What it does, for whom, and how we know it works. No parts and no numbers.*

## What it does

Four finders in one hand, for whoever must know what is in a wall, a floor or a board before
touching it. Everything a professional carries in several tools, in one head on the Báze.

**One tool**, held and used like a metal detector, **one mode at a time**, chosen by the user.

1. **The thread.** It finds where a cable runs — along a wall, through a floor, across a
   building — by the signal on it: the mains at 50 Hz, or the signal of a cable tracer the user
   already has, plugged in somewhere (33 kHz, 120 kHz, whatever the systems use). **No sender of
   its own:** the receiver takes 50 Hz to about 130 kHz and picks out the frequencies in software.
   It reads the cable two ways, **by its charge** (a capacitive plate) and **by its current**
   (three small ferrite rods at right angles, x, y and z), so it finds a cable shorted further
   on, which a voltage probe cannot. Klubok, the magic ball of the tales, rolls ahead and leads
   the way; the signal is the thread.
2. **Metal.** With a field of its own it finds metal near the surface, and tells iron from
   copper. No sender needed.
3. **Voltage.** The plainest: a sound, or a bar that grows, where the mains voltage is. No sender
   needed.
4. **Heat.** **Gradusnik**: the Báze's ordinary camera and its thermal one, their pictures laid
   half over each other, so that what is warm is seen where it is — the cable that carries a load,
   the terminal that is loose in a board of a thousand wires.

## What the thread does that the bought ones do not

- **It shows a place, not a loudness** — where the cable is, which way it runs and, from a few
  points, how deep, from the direction of its field in three axes, not only a bar that grows.
- **It listens to every tracer at once**, each on its own frequency, so two cables with two
  senders are told apart, and it is of use beside the tracer a tradesman already has.
- **It hears what the mains carries** without any sender, by the charge and by the current.

All four are heard or seen, and all are open: every setting and the raw signal are the
user's.

## For whom

The electrician who must find one circuit among many in a building with no drawings, and whoever
has stood with a bought tracer in front of a wall where everything lights up.

## What it is not

- Not a voltage tester: it never says that a cable is dead.
- Not a picture of the inside of a wall — that is Rys. It follows a cable by its signal, and says
  of metal, voltage and heat that they are here.

## How we know it works

1. On a cable whose route is known, the receiver leads along that route and nowhere else.
2. Over the cable it shows the place and the direction; a neighbouring cable a hand's width away
   is not taken for it.
3. In a building where a bought tracer lights up every socket, it tells the cable from the rest.
4. Two tracers on two cables are told apart by their frequencies.
5. It finds a nail, a pipe and a cable's copper under plaster, and says which is iron.
6. It sounds over a live cable in a dry wall.
7. Over a loaded cable in an open board the thermal picture shows which one, laid where the
   ordinary picture has it.
8. A person who did not build it does all this from the instructions.
